Most investing content makes it sound like the hard part is picking the right stock or timing the market correctly. For most people, neither of those is actually the job. A small number of low-cost index funds, left alone for a couple of decades, does most of the heavy lifting.

## What an index fund actually is

An index fund is a basket of investments built to track a market index, something like "the 500 largest US companies," rather than trying to beat it. Buying one share means owning a small slice of hundreds or thousands of companies at once, instead of betting on any single one of them.

<Callout type="note">
  Index funds won't make anyone rich quickly. They're built for steady, long-term growth, which is the entire point rather than a limitation.
</Callout>

## Why simplicity tends to win

| Approach | Typical annual fee | Requires ongoing research |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Actively picking stocks | Varies widely | Yes, continuously |
| Actively managed funds | 0.5%–1.5% | Delegated, but you pay for it |
| Broad index funds | 0.03%–0.10% | No |

That fee difference looks small on paper, but it compounds the same way returns do. Take $10,000 invested for 30 years: growing at 7% a year, it becomes roughly $76,000. Growing at 6%, just one percentage point lower to account for higher fees, it becomes roughly $57,000. Same starting amount, same time period, and the fee alone accounts for nearly $19,000 of the difference. Fees are one of the few variables in investing that's fully within your control, which is exactly why they're worth paying attention to.

## A simple starting approach

1. **Open a tax-advantaged account first** if you have access to one, like a workplace retirement plan or an IRA. The tax treatment alone is worth more than most other optimizations combined.
2. **Choose one or two broad, low-cost index funds.** A total market or S&P 500 fund covers a lot of ground on its own.
3. **Set up automatic monthly contributions**, so investing doesn't depend on remembering to do it or feeling confident about the market that particular week.
4. **Leave it alone.** Resist the urge to check daily. Nothing about checking more often improves the outcome, and it usually just adds stress.

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't try to time your entry">
  Waiting for the "right moment" to start usually costs more than starting imperfectly today and staying consistent. Nobody, including professional fund managers, reliably calls market tops and bottoms.
</Callout>

## What this approach isn't

This isn't a claim that active investing or individual stock picking never works. Some people do genuinely enjoy researching companies and are willing to accept the extra risk and time commitment that comes with it. It's a claim that for most people, most of the time, the version of investing that requires the least ongoing effort also happens to have a strong long-term track record, which is a rare combination. The best investing plan is usually the one boring enough to actually stick with for twenty years.
