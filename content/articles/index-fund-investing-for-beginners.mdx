Investing doesn't have to mean picking winning stocks or timing the market. For most people, a small number of low-cost index funds does the heavy lifting.

## What an index fund actually is

An index fund is a basket of investments designed to track a market index — like "the 500 largest US companies" — rather than trying to beat it. You own a small slice of hundreds or thousands of companies at once.

<Callout type="note">
  Index funds won't make you rich quickly. They're built for steady,
  long-term growth — which is exactly the point.
</Callout>

## Why simplicity tends to win

| Approach | Typical annual fee | Requires ongoing research |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Actively picking stocks | Varies widely | Yes, continuously |
| Actively managed funds | 0.5%–1.5% | Delegated, but you pay for it |
| Broad index funds | 0.03%–0.10% | No |

Lower fees compound in your favor the same way returns do — a 1% difference in annual fees can mean tens of thousands of dollars over a few decades.

## A simple starting approach

1. Open a tax-advantaged account first (like a workplace retirement plan or IRA) if you have access to one
2. Choose one or two broad, low-cost index funds
3. Set up automatic monthly contributions
4. Leave it alone — resist the urge to check daily

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't try to time your entry">
  Waiting for the "right moment" to start investing usually costs more than
  starting imperfectly today and staying consistent.
</Callout>

The best investing plan is one boring enough that you'll actually stick with it for twenty years.
