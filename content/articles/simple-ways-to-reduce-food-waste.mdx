The average household throws out roughly a fifth of the food it buys. That's not an abstract environmental statistic. It's produce that went slimy in the crisper drawer, bread that went stale before the second half got used, leftovers that got pushed to the back and forgotten. All of it was paid for. None of it got eaten.

None of the fixes here require much extra effort. Most of them take less time than the shopping trip that brought the food home in the first place.

## Start with an "eat this first" shelf

Designate one shelf in the fridge for anything close to its use-by date. Nothing fancy, just a consistent spot checked before reaching for something new. The whole idea is visibility: food that's easy to see gets eaten, food buried behind everything else gets forgotten and thrown out three weeks later.

<Callout type="tip">
  Put this shelf at eye level, not in a drawer. Food that's out of sight gets forgotten, and forgotten food gets wasted.
</Callout>

## Plan meals around what's already there

Before writing a grocery list, a five-minute inventory of the fridge and pantry changes what ends up on it. Build two or three meals around what's already on hand, then shop to fill in the gaps rather than starting from a blank list every week.

- Check the fridge, freezer, and pantry first, before opening any recipe site or app
- Plan meals that share overlapping ingredients, so nothing bought for one recipe goes unused
- Buy only what those meals actually need, resisting the extra "just in case" items

## Freeze more than seems reasonable

Bread, milk, fresh herbs, and even cooked rice all freeze better than most people expect. Herbs specifically can be chopped and frozen in a little water or oil, then dropped straight into whatever's cooking later. If something's about to turn and won't get used in the next day or two, freezing it is almost always better than letting it go in the trash.

<Callout type="note" title="Not sure if it's still good?">
  "Best by" dates are about peak quality, not safety. Use your senses first: smell, texture, appearance, before assuming something needs to go. A lot of perfectly fine food gets thrown out purely because of a date on the package.
</Callout>

## What this actually adds up to

None of these habits are dramatic on their own. Together, they touch nearly every source of food waste in an average kitchen: forgotten leftovers, produce that spoiled before it got used, and the extra items bought without a plan. A household that cuts food waste meaningfully tends to notice it first at the grocery store, not in a sense of virtue about the environment, just a bill that's quietly a bit smaller than it used to be.
