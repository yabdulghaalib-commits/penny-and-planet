Somewhere around the fifth open browser tab comparing supply chains for a pair of socks, most people give up on ethical shopping entirely. Researching the full backstory of every purchase doesn't lead to better decisions. It leads to exhaustion, and then to buying whatever's easiest out of sheer decision fatigue. A simpler framework holds up better over time.

## Buy less, first

The single most reliably "ethical" purchase is the one that doesn't happen. Before researching a brand's supply chain, it's worth asking whether the item is needed at all, or whether something already owned solves the same problem. This sounds obvious written down, but it's the step most guides skip in favor of recommending a better brand instead of a smaller cart.

<Callout type="tip">
  Try a 48-hour rule for non-essential purchases. Most of the guilt around "unethical" shopping comes from impulse buys that weren't needed in the first place, not from the brand's labor practices.
</Callout>

Here's what that looks like in practice: something catches your eye online. Instead of buying it immediately, add it to a list and revisit in two days. Often the impulse fades entirely. When it doesn't, at least the decision gets made with a clear head instead of a dopamine hit.

## When you do need to buy

- **Prioritize durability over trend.** A well-made item worn for years beats a "sustainable" item replaced every season, regardless of what either one is made from.
- **Buy secondhand where reasonable.** It's often the most genuinely sustainable option available, since it requires no new resources at all, and it's usually the cheapest option too.
- **Pick two or three causes that actually matter to you** (labor conditions, materials, packaging, animal welfare) rather than trying to optimize for everything at once. Trying to satisfy every ethical criterion simultaneously is how the guilt spiral starts in the first place.

## What "good enough" actually looks like

Nobody shops perfectly ethically, including people who write about it for a living. A reasonable target: most purchases are things actually needed, bought to last, from a source that isn't actively terrible on the one or two issues that matter most personally. That's a workable standard most weeks. An ideal one, chased every single time, isn't.

Perfect ethical consumption doesn't exist. A realistic framework that actually gets followed beats an ideal one abandoned after a month of exhausting research.
