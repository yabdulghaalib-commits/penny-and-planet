A $180 pair of boots sounds expensive until they've been worn twice a week for three years. At around 300 wears, that's roughly 60 cents a wear, cheaper than almost anything bought to replace them. A $25 shirt worn twice before it gets shoved to the back of the closet costs $12.50 a wear: the more expensive item, even though it cost seven times less upfront.

That's cost-per-wear: what something cost, divided by how many times it actually got used. It's a better way to think about value than the price tag alone, and it tends to reward a smaller wardrobe rather than simply a cheaper one.

## Why sticker price is the wrong number to optimize

Price alone says what something costs to buy. It says nothing about what it costs to own, because it ignores how often it actually gets worn. A well-made coat worn every winter for a decade is a better financial decision than five cheap coats bought and discarded over the same period, even though the cheap coats individually cost less.

<Callout type="note">
  This isn't an argument for buying expensive things. It's an argument for buying things that will actually get worn a lot, which sometimes means spending more, and sometimes means not buying anything at all.
</Callout>

## How to actually find your cost-per-wear pieces

No app or spreadsheet is required for this. A month of paying attention does most of the work.

- **Track what gets reached for.** For a few weeks, notice which pieces get worn without a second thought. That list is usually short, and more honest than an aspirational sense of personal style.
- **Notice what doesn't get worn.** Clothes with tags still on, or that haven't left the closet in six months, are the opposite signal.
- **Replace only what's genuinely worn out**, not whatever caught the eye most recently.
- **Choose neutrals that mix with more of what's already owned.** A piece that works with everything gets worn more often, which is the entire point of this exercise.

## The honest tradeoff

Building a wardrobe this way takes longer than buying whatever's on sale, and it sometimes means spending more upfront on fewer things. It isn't the fastest way to fill a closet. Over a few years, though, it's usually the cheaper approach, and it tends to make getting dressed simpler instead of harder. There's less to choose from, and less of it turns out to be a mistake still being paid for.
