A bag of dried lentils costs about the same as a candy bar and turns into six or seven servings of dinner. That's the part of plant-based eating that doesn't show up in articles about $8 oat-milk lattes and $12 frozen "plant-based" meals: the ingredients that actually make up most plant-based cooking are some of the cheapest food in the grocery store.

The expensive version of plant-based eating exists. It's just not the only version, and it's usually not the one worth choosing on a budget.

## What actually keeps the cost down

Beans, lentils, rice, oats, and seasonal vegetables are the backbone of cheap plant-based cooking, and they'd be cheap even if nobody called them "plant-based" at all. A pound of dried lentils runs a couple of dollars and cooks up to roughly six cups. Compare that to a pound of ground beef, and the gap is significant, especially once you factor in that lentils don't shrink on the stove the way meat does.

<Callout type="tip">
  Buy dried beans and lentils instead of canned when the extra 30 minutes of cooking works for your schedule. They cost a fraction as much per serving, and unlike canned versions, they keep almost indefinitely in the pantry.
</Callout>

## Where people overspend on purpose

The expensive part of plant-based eating is almost always the pre-made substitutes: veggie burgers, plant-based "chicken," dairy-free cheese trying to taste like the real thing. These products are convenient, and there's nothing wrong with buying them occasionally. But priced per serving, most of them cost close to what real meat and dairy cost, sometimes more. If saving money is the goal, that's not where the savings live.

## A simple weekly rotation

A big repertoire isn't necessary to eat well on this budget. Three anchor meals cover most weeks:

1. **A big pot of lentil or bean soup**, portioned into containers for three or four days
2. **A grain bowl** built around whatever vegetables are cheapest that week, plus rice or another grain and a simple sauce
3. **One "effort" meal on the weekend**, when there's more time to cook something with more steps

<Callout type="note">
  Skip the pre-made meat alternatives if budget is genuinely the priority. They're usually priced closer to meat than to the whole ingredients they're made from, which defeats the purpose if cost is what brought you here.
</Callout>

## Where this doesn't apply

None of this means every plant-based product is a waste of money. If a busy week means the choice is a frozen veggie burger or takeout, the burger is still the cheaper and often the more sustainable option. The point isn't to avoid convenience food entirely. It's to notice that convenience, not "plant-based" itself, is what you're paying extra for.

Plant-based eating and frugal eating overlap more than most food media suggests, mostly because the cheapest ingredients in any grocery store tend to be plants. The move isn't finding a clever substitute for meat. It's building meals around the vegetables, grains, and legumes that were already the inexpensive option to begin with.
